# Structured Causal Video Reasoning via Multi-Objective Alignment

Zinuo Li  Yongxin Guo  Jun Liu  Jiawei Zhan  Xi Jiang  Chengjie Wang  Mohammed Bennamoun  Farid Boussaid  Feng Zheng  Qiuhong Ke 

###### Abstract

Human understanding of video dynamics is typically grounded in a structured mental representation of entities, actions, and temporal relations, rather than relying solely on immediate deductive reasoning. In contrast, existing Video-LLMs largely depend on unstructured video reasoning, where critical visual evidence is embedded in verbose textual descriptions and temporal causality is often weakly modeled. This leads to inefficient processes and fragile causal inference. To bridge this cognitive gap, we propose constructing a compact representation of salient events and their causal relationships, which we name Structured Event Facts, prior to the reasoning stage. This structured prior serves as an explicit constraint to promote concise and causally grounded reasoning, while also making intermediate evidence easier to verify. To effectively train models on such structured facts, we introduce CausalFact-60K and a four-stage training pipeline comprising facts alignment, format warm-start, thinking warm-start, and reinforcement learning-based post-training. During RL stage, we find that this framework introduces competing objectives, as structural completeness and causal fidelity must be balanced against reasoning length, making it difficult to optimize. We address this challenge by formulating the optimization as a Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL) problem and explicitly optimizing toward the Pareto-Frontier to balance these trade-offs. As a result, we introduce Factum-4B, which yields more reliable reasoning and delivers stronger performance on challenging video understanding tasks requiring fine-grained temporal inference.

Video Understanding 

##  1 Introduction

Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) have achieved remarkable success in interpreting static images by adapting the architectural paradigms of Large Language Models (LLMs) (Liu et al., 2024a; Bai et al., 2023). Recently, this progress has extended to the video domain, aiming to capture dynamic temporal information (Zhang et al., 2023a; Li et al., 2024). To enhance the reasoning capabilities of these models, the research community has predominantly adopted the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) paradigm (Wei et al., 2022) in Large Language Models, which encourages models to generate intermediate reasoning steps before deriving a final answer. Later on, CoT has also been proven effective to multimodal tasks (Bai et al., 2025a).

Figure 1: Compared with existing video reasoning approaches, our model first extracts facts event information from videos. It then applies a thinking process strictly constrained by causal relationships, which is specifically optimized for video data. This method clarifies critical information and enhances interpretability while focusing on the temporal dimension of videos. Video is sampled from ActivityNet-Captions (Krishna et al., 2017). 

Unlike text, video data is characterized by dense spatio-temporal redundancy. When models simply employ unstructured CoT like text-only tasks, the resulting generation often devolves into verbose, non-linear narratives where pivotal visual cues are drowned in irrelevant tokens. Empirically, we observe that reasoning-enhanced Thinking models often degrade in performance compared to their Instruction-tuned counterparts (Bai et al., 2025a). They suffer from reasoning drift, losing focus on the user query, and frequently reduce the temporal dimension to isolated frame retrieval rather than analyzing causal relationships. This behavior contrasts sharply with human cognitive processes. Psychological research suggests that human perception relies on forming a structured mental sketch, which registers entities, actions and event boundaries before engaging in abstract reasoning (Zacks and Swallow, 2007).

Motivated by this cognitive mechanism, we propose a Structure-First paradigm, a module generated prior to the reasoning phase. As shown in Figure 1, we enforce the model to synthesize a high-density schema containing time, object, human action, scene, and causal event caption before the reasoning process begins. This structured prior serves as an explicit constraint, anchoring the subsequent reasoning to be concise, salient, and causally grounded.

However, there is often a trade-off between maintaining structural strictness and ensuring reasoning length. We find that this multi-stage reasoning process presents a non-trivial optimization challenge during Reinforcement Learning (RL) stage. In resource-constrained settings with limited length budgets, maximizing facts density can inadvertently truncate reasoning depth, and vice versa. Standard RL strategies (Christiano et al., 2017), typically optimized via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman and others, 2017), or Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) struggle to balance these conflicting rewards, leading to training instability, where the advantages calculation cannot distinguish optimization direction.

To avoid collapsing multiple rewards into a single scalar, which can obscure critical trade-offs, we treat multi-dimensional reward vectors as distinct signals for policy updates. Unlike standard GRPO, we treat each reward component as an independent objective to preserve task-specific gradients. Inspired by the Multiple Gradient Descent Algorithm (MGDA) (Désidéri, 2012), we propose Pareto-Frontier guided Advantage Balancing (P-FAB) algorithm to effectively resolve rewards conflicts and dynamically balance distinct objectives. Our contributions are summarized as follows:

  * •

We identify the limitations of unstructured CoT in video understanding. To address this, we propose CausalFact-60K, a dataset that empowers the model to precisely output facts information and utilize causal relationships for video reasoning with dense captions.

  * •

We reveal that the standard GRPO advantage calculation may lead to conflicts among multi-objective rewards and ambiguous gradient directions. Motivated by the pareto-frontier, we propose Pareto-Frontier guided Advantage Balancing (P-FAB) algorithm. This algorithm dynamically balances multi-objective advantages based on pareto-optimal during the advantage calculation process.

  * •

Based on aforementioned contributions, we train a small but strong model: Factum-4B. Extensive experiments on standard benchmarks demonstrate that Factum-4B significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, validating the effectiveness of structured reasoning process and P-FAB algorithm.

##  2 Related Work

##### Video understanding LVLMs.

Following the success of image-to-text LVLMs (Liu et al., 2024a; Dai et al., 2023), early video LLMs treated videos as sequences of static frames and employed token compression to fit high-dimensional spatio-temporal signals into limited LLM token budgets (Maaz et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023a). However, this frame-level approach overlooks core challenges in video understanding, particularly temporal grounding and causal reasoning (Liu et al., 2024c; Chen et al., 2024). To address this, recent works have introduced time-aware architectures (Ren et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024, 2025) to better capture temporal dynamics, or incorporated memory and tool-augmented mechanisms for handling long-form, untrimmed videos (Song et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Parallel efforts focus on improving training data quality and scale (Zhang et al., 2024) and establishing reliable benchmarks for evaluating long-horizon temporal and causal reasoning (Mangalam et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2024). Yet, despite architectural advances, current video LLMs often rely on sparse frame heuristics rather than modeling true event dynamics (Mangalam et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2021), limiting their performance in complex real-world scenarios. Our method addresses this by learning an explicit structured prior that summarizes key events and their causal relationships before free-form reasoning, effectively enhancing temporal grounding and reducing dependence on brittle frame-selection shortcuts.

##### Reasoning and chain-of-thought in LVLMs.

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022) improves LLM reasoning by eliciting intermediate steps, and Multimodal-CoT extends this to vision-language models by conditioning reasoning traces on visual features (Zhang et al., 2023b). However, visual inputs are often both redundant, containing many irrelevant frames, also underspecified with fine-grained temporal and causal cues easily missed. As a result, unconstrained vision Chain of Thought tends to become verbose, drift off-topic, or hallucinate unsupported details, especially when intermediate steps lack grounding in verifiable evidence (Chen et al., 2023). Prior approaches address this through tool-augmented verification (e.g., ReAct/MM-ReAct) (Yao et al., 2023b; Yang et al., 2023), exploration of multiple reasoning paths (e.g., Tree-of-Thoughts, self-consistency) (Yao et al., 2023a; Wang et al., 2023). In contrast, we generate an internal _Structured Event Facts_ , a compact, high-density schema encoding key events, entities, and causal relationships, which explicitly constrains subsequent CoT reasoning to be concise, salient, and firmly grounded in extracted video facts.

##### Reinforcement learning in LVLMs

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) aligns LLMs with human preferences using comparison-based feedback (Ouyang and et al., 2022; Touvron and et al., 2023), typically via scalar rewards optimized with PPO (Schulman and others, 2017). Recent work proposes GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), a more efficient alternative that updates policies using group-wise relative comparisons, reducing reliance on value-function estimation while enabling rule-based rewards modeling. For LVLMs, existing approaches directly apply GRPO to diverse video understanding tasks such as reasoning, temporal grounding, and counting (Feng et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). However, these methods overlook the inherently multi-objective nature of video understanding, where models must balance objectives including structural constraints, reasoning capability, final accuracy, and computational budgets, etc. In this paper, rather than naively averaging rewards, we optimize reward weights by solving a Multi-Gradient Descent Algorithm (MGDA) objective to approximate the Pareto-Frontier (Kong et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b). This approach ensures a fair, stable, and balanced optimization across all objectives.

##  3 Methodology

###  3.1 Overview

To reformulate complex video thinking, a straightforward approach would typically involve a two-stage process: Thinking Warm-start to establish causal logic, followed by Reinforcement Learning for alignment. However, the unique integration of Structured Event Facts within our framework necessitates a specialized orchestration of the training steps.

We empirically find that this facts-first dependency introduces an optimization barrier. Directly optimizing accurate factual description and complex causal reasoning through these two simple stages overwhelms the model, leading to hallucinated facts or collapsed reasoning structures. To resolve this conflict, we decouple the learning process into four progressive stages: Facts Training (Stage 1), Format Warm-Start (Stage 1.5), Thinking Warm-Start (Stage 2), and RL-based Post-training (Stage 3).

Stage 1 trains the model to produce high-quality facts outputs with accurate descriptions of person, action, scene, etc. Stage 2 focuses on training the model’s causal reasoning ability. These two stages are both trained through instruction tuning. In Stage 1, we ask the model to output facts information from the video, while in Stage 2, we ask real QA questions such as temporal grounding and visual reasoning. Detailed prompts are shown in the Appendix B.4.

Notably, to bridge the gap between pure factual description (Stage 1) and complex reasoning (Stage 2), we introduce an intermediate Stage 1.5. In this stage, we ask for the same facts as in Stage 1 but require the model to strictly adhere to the thinking format (e.g., <thinking>...<answering>), thereby warming up the structural format. Otherwise, the model hallucinates bad structure. Finally, Stage 3 employs our proposed Pareto-Frontier guided Advantage Balancing (P-FAB) to resolve the objective conflicts in multi-objective reinforcement learning. Since each stage requires distinct supervision signals, we construct a comprehensive data curation pipeline, detailed in Section 3.2.

###  3.2 Data Curation: Facts and Thinking

####  3.2.1 Motivation

Although video reasoning has raised researchers’ attention, existing video understanding models often reason without structure (Wang et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2025a), where critical clues are submerged in redundant text. Conventional Chain-of-Thought (CoT) can become verbose and unfocused on long videos, introducing invalid steps while under-emphasizing temporal causal logic in favor of discrete time points.

This motivates us to propose a thinking process with clearer clues, better interpretability, and stronger causality. Thus, we propose to model the video understanding problem as follows: Facts, Thinking, Answering, where a clear reasoning process with strong causality can be established.

Specifically, we force the model to output Structured Event Facts as the primary step of our methodology to enhance structured information extraction, aiming to provide precise, compact, and query-relevant clues that establish a reliable semantic foundation for subsequent reasoning. In this stage, the model is required to describe the major events in the video at reasonable intervals, spanning approximately 10 to 30 seconds, and extract the key cues within each event, such as salient characters, human actions, scene context, and camera position, and then provide a detailed, time-ordered description of each event in chronological sequence, as illustrated Figure 2.

Building upon the resulting Structured Event Facts, we aim to steer the model toward better causal reasoning. Leveraging the salient clues extracted in the facts phase, we enforce a structured causal reasoning process with three tightly coupled stages: 1. Global Search & Localization, 2. Causal Verification, and 3. Final Alignment.

As shown in Figure 2. The first phase Global Search & Localization requires the model to retrieve query-relevant clues from the structured facts list to quickly narrow attention to a small set of highly relevant time windows, providing a reliable starting point for downstream reasoning. In Causal Verification stage, it examines temporal coherence by analyzing the preceding and succeeding events around the hypothesized interval, and validates the localization through cause, which effects consistency rather than shallow pattern matching. To avoid over-reliance on textual priors and keep the decision grounded in visual evidence, the model performs a mandatory visual verification on the inferred interval using vision tokens sent with textual prompts.

In Final Alignment stage, the model performs a global consistency check to ensure the inferred temporal interval is fully contained within the observed activity sequences, improving the overall reliability of the reasoning process.

Figure 3: Overview of the two-stage pipeline for constructing from VTG datasets. Stage 1 performs video filtering and gap filling, generates structured facts captions, and applies an automatic quality judge with random human inspection; low-quality samples are rejected and iteratively refined. Stage 2 produces causally grounded reasoning traces, followed by a second quality-judging and human spot-checking step, yielding the final curated causal–facts set. 

####  3.2.2 Data Construction Pipeline

As illustrated in Figure 3, to enable high-quality facts and Thinking generation, we build a comprehensive data construction pipeline.

For pre-processing, we adopt a strict selection pipeline to distill informative samples from high-quality human-annotated Video Temporal Grounding datasets (Gao et al., 2017; Krishna et al., 2017). More video source details are shown in the Appendix B. We prioritize videos with high event density, assuming an ideal event segment spans 20–30 seconds: for a video of duration TT, we retain it only if event number N≥T/30N\geq T/30, i.e., a 150-second video requires at least 5 events. For retained videos, we further apply gap filling to improve continuity: unlabeled intervals between adjacent annotated events (e.g., unlabeled [25,38][25,38] between annotated [15,25][15,25] and [38,50][38,50]) are treated as candidate event segments for the model to describe, increasing coverage and encouraging global spatiotemporal modeling. Videos with N<T/30N\textless T/30 are excluded from facts training to avoid weakening structured signals, but are retained for the subsequent RL stage to enhance robustness and generalization.

Next, we feed the filtered dense timestamps into a facts Generator to produce the event facts descriptions. Since facts are fine-grained structured signals that require strong instruction following to achieve high data quality, we employ two complementary models: Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025a) and Gemini2.5- Pro (Comanici et al., 2025). To prevent specific model preferences from compromising data quality, the two models alternate roles: when one serves as the generator, the other acts as a quality judge. The two strong models mutually critique and filter outputs, enabling facts to capture detailed visual cues. The data produced in this stage is served for Stage 1 and Stage 1.5.

After obtaining the facts, we generate challenging questions and prompt the LLM to produce explicit thinking traces conditioned on the question and the associated facts information. Since thinking data demands a higher quality than facts itself, we use Gemini2.5-Pro exclusively as the generator in this stage, while Qwen3-VL serves as the quality judge. The data produced in this stage is served for Stage 2.

To ensure data quality, we conduct a human evaluation by sampling 50% of the total 60K annotations. Specifically, we partition the generated data into batches of 1,000 instances and randomly select 500 samples from each batch for manual inspection.

###  3.3 Pareto-Frontier guided Advantage Balancing

##### Motivations.

After the first three stages, the model acquires structured reasoning capabilities. In Stage 3, we introduce reinforcement learning to align the model with real-world problem-solving. However, the integration of facts, thinking, and answering components naturally elongates the chain-of-thought, transforming the training into a complex Multi-Objective Optimization problem, where two problems may occur in standard Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO):

(1) Inherent Conflicts: There is a natural Pareto trade-off between comprehensive fact extraction and token efficiency, which static weights cannot dynamically navigate; and (2) Directional Ambiguity: Standard GRPO cannot distinguish update differences between multiple objectives. As illustrated in Figure 4, GRPO produces identical advantage values for candidates with the same average rewards, thereby masking the underlying distribution and potential imbalances among different reward components.

Figure 4: GRPO vs P-FAB advantage comparison. P-FAB dynamically adjusts weights by solving a minimum-norm problem in the standardized reward space, ensuring that rare but critical signals are not overwhelmed by high-variance conflicting objectives.

##### Pitfalls of traditional policy gradient optimization.

In policy gradient optimization, updates follow the direction A⋅∇θlog⁡πθ​(oi∣qi)A\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}\mid q_{i}), where the advantage AA is derived from a weighted reward Rfinal=∑wi​riR_{\text{final}}=\sum w_{i}r_{i}. To manage conflicting objectives, a common practice is to tune the coefficients wiw_{i} to prioritize specific goals. However, since these weights remain static during training, they often exacerbate trade-offs rather than resolving the underlying conflicts.

##### P-FAB as a solution.

We reformulate this interaction by defining a group-wide reward vector 𝐯m=[r1,m,…,rG,m]⊤∈ℝG\mathbf{v}_{m}=[r_{1,m},\dots,r_{G,m}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{G} for each objective mm, where GG is the number of responses per prompt. We interpret each 𝐯m\mathbf{v}_{m} as a “directional preference vector”, in essence a “vote” from objective mm on which samples to promote or suppress. By stacking these update signals into a matrix Δ\Delta and solving for the minimum-norm combination within its convex hull, we derive a compromise update that is maximally compatible with all objectives. Inspired by MGDA (Désidéri, 2012), we propose Pareto-Frontier guided Advantage Balancing (P-FAB) algorithm, as described in Algorithm 1.

##### Methodology Details.

Following the group-relative approach in GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), we first isolate the relative quality of each completion within its prompt group. Consider a batch ℬ\mathcal{B} containing NN prompts, where each prompt qq generates a group of GG responses {o1,…,oG}\\{o_{1},\dots,o_{G}\\}. For each objective mm, the centered reward δi,m\delta_{i,m} for response oio_{i} is computed by subtracting the group mean:

| δi,m=rm​(q,oi)−1G​∑j=1Grm​(q,oj).\delta_{i,m}=r_{m}(q,o_{i})-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}r_{m}(q,o_{j}). |  | (1)  
---|---|---|---  
  
This normalization ensures that δi,m\delta_{i,m} indicates whether response oio_{i} outperforms the group average for objective mm.

##### Solving Common Direction.

The theoretical foundation of our approach stems from MGDA. In multi-objective optimization, the goal is to find a parameter update that simultaneously improves all objectives {L1​(ω),…,LM​(ω)}\\{L_{1}(\omega),\ldots,L_{M}(\omega)\\}. MGDA achieves this by seeking a convex combination of task gradients that forms a common descent direction. A key result from MGDA theory shows that finding such a direction is equivalent to solving the following dual problem:

| min𝜶∈ΔM⁡‖∑m=1Mαm​∇Lm​(ω)‖2.\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta_{M}}\left\|\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}\nabla L_{m}(\omega)\right\|^{2}. |  | (2)  
---|---|---|---  
  
When the optimal norm is zero, a Pareto-stationary point is reached, otherwise, the minimizing combination yields a descent direction that benefits all tasks. In our preference optimization setting, the centered reward δi,m\delta_{i,m} defined in Eq. (1) plays an analogous role to task gradients: it represents the per-sample, per-objective signal indicating how the policy should be updated. This analogy allows us to adapt the MGDA principle to our setting: by minimizing the norm of the weighted combination ∑mαm​δi,m\sum_{m}\alpha_{m}\delta_{i,m}, we seek a consensus direction that balances all reward objectives.

However, a primary challenge in applying MGDA directly to δi,m\delta_{i,m} is scale sensitivity: objectives with high variance can dominate the norm minimization, suppressing signals from other tasks. To ensure scale invariance, P-FAB computes optimal weights in a standardized space. For a prompt group qq, let 𝐃q\mathbf{D}_{q} be the matrix with entries δi,m\delta_{i,m}. We construct the standardized matrix 𝐃^q\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q} by scaling each column by its standard deviation σq,m\sigma_{q,m}, ignoring objectives where σq,m<τ=1​e−6\sigma_{q,m}<\tau=1e-6 to maintain numerical stability.

##### Discussion on P-FAB.

As illustrated in Figure 4, P-FAB 1) naturally amplifies sparse and hard-to-satisfy objectives while reducing the weights of rewards that are more easily obtained. Consequently, the resulting advantage values are more discriminative, prioritizing samples that satisfy critical, rare criteria over those that merely perform well on simpler tasks. 2) We consider the model to have reached a local Pareto-Frontier when the objectives achieve Pareto stationarity. In this state, the convex hull of the task gradients contains the origin (min𝜶⁡‖∑αm​∇ℒm‖≈0\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|\sum\alpha_{m}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{m}\|\approx 0), implying that no common descent direction exists. At this point, any further improvement in one objective necessitates a degradation in another, signifying that the model has fully navigated the inherent trade-offs of the task.

##  4 Experiments

###  4.1 Implementation Details

We use Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct as our base model. Since the 4B model is more susceptible to general-capability forgetting, we adopt stage-specific hyperparameter settings across our three training stages. For Stage 1, Stage 1.5, and Stage 2, we fine-tune the model with LoRA, but with different hyperparameters. In Stage 1 and Stage 1.5, we use LoRA with rank 6464 and α=128\alpha=128, a learning rate of 1×10−51\times 10^{-5}, and weight decay of 1×10−41\times 10^{-4} to mitigate catastrophic forgetting of general knowledge. In Stage 2, we keep the same LoRA setup but increase the learning rate to 1×10−41\times 10^{-4} and weight decay to 2×10−42\times 10^{-4}.

For the RL Stage 3, we use 4 different rewards: Format, Linear IoU, Multi-choice Accuracy, and Length, where Length reward makes the conflicts. Detailed reward designs are shown in the Appendix D.1. We perform full-parameter updates with a learning rate of 5×10−65\times 10^{-6}, num​_​generation=8\mathrm{num\\_generation=8}, video max tokens set to 6464. We set video​_​fps=1.0\mathrm{video\\_fps}=1.0 and epoch=1.0\mathrm{epoch}=1.0 across all stages, with video​_​max​_​frames=256\mathrm{video\\_max\\_frames}=256 for Stage1, Stage1.5 and Stage2, while deceasing to video​_​max​_​frames=128\mathrm{video\\_max\\_frames}=128 for Stage3 to save memory. During the evaluation, we re-evaluate Qwen3 series based on the same settings of ours, i.e., fps=1\mathrm{fps=1} and max​_​frames=256\mathrm{max\\_frames}=256. For other models, their results come from the official reports, typically with fps=2\mathrm{fps=2} and max​_​frames=2048\mathrm{max\\_frames}=2048.

###  4.2 Ablation Studies

We evaluate component effectiveness on Charades-TimeLens (Zhang et al., 2025a) and ActivityNet-Captions (Krishna et al., 2017) for temporal grounding, and VideoMME (Fu et al., 2024) and MLVU (Zhou et al., 2025) for general understanding. We compare our full model against five variants: (1) w/o Facts excludes factual descriptions to perform direct structured thinking; (2) w/o Thinking generates facts but predicts answers without causal reasoning; (3) w/o RL utilizes only supervised fine-tuning; and (4-5) GRPO/P-FAB, comparing standard GRPO against our P-FAB with num​_​generation=4​or​8\mathrm{num\\_generation=4~or~8}. Detailed dataset introductions are shown in the Appendix A.

As shown in Table 1, both of the facts information and structured thinking are indispensable. Removing the thinking process (w/o Thinking) leads to the most severe performance collapse across all benchmarks, notably dropping to 58.5% on VideoMME. This failure stems from the model’s inability to effectively utilize the extracted facts. Without a reasoning bridge, the causal link between factual perception and the final answer is severed, rendering the facts underutilized and disconnected from the objective. Similarly, omitting structured facts (w/o Facts) significantly impairs performance, as these facts serve as clear cues that guide the thinking process. Without this grounding foundation, the reasoning chain frequently enters logical pitfalls or misguided paths, confirming that verified facts act as a critical anchor to steer the thinking process away from misconceptions.

Regarding the optimization strategy, our P-FAB consistently outperforms standard GRPO. Crucially, while P-FAB already shows clear gains at a group size of 4, the performance gap between P-FAB and standard GRPO widens significantly when the group size is increased to 8. For instance, on ActivityNet R1@0.3, the margin between P-FAB (our full model) and GRPO grows from 1.2% at G=4 to 2.5% at G=8. This suggests that P-FAB possesses superior optimization dynamics in larger group settings, where it can more effectively navigate the expanded sampling space to resolve complex multi-objective conflicts and more fully manifest its advantages. Finally, our RL post-training yields a substantial 8.3% gain on ActivityNet R1@0.3 compared to the w/o RL baseline, with our full model achieving state-of-the-art results across both temporal grounding and general reasoning tasks.

###  4.3 Comparisons with Existing Video-LLMs

We evaluate Factum-4B against state-of-the-art close-source and open-source Video-LLMs across both temporal grounding and general video understanding benchmarks. As summarized in Table 2 and Table 3, our model demonstrates a significant performance leap, particularly in fine-grained temporal reasoning and causal deduction.

Leading Performance in Temporal Grounding. Factum-4B sets a new state-of-the-art among open-source models for temporal localization. Despite operating at a lower frame rate (1fps), it consistently outperforms 7B-scale models such as Time-R1-7B and VideoChat-R1-7B. On ActivityNet-Captions, our model achieves a substantial R1@0.5 of 48.4% and R1@0.7 of 28.1%, significantly surpassing Time-R1-7B. Furthermore, on the high-precision Charades-TimeLens benchmark, Factum-4B reaches an R1@0.7 of 21.6%, the highest among all open-source candidates. Compared to Qwen3-VL-4B-Thinking, our model improves the R1@0.3 on ActivityNet-TimeLens from 48.2% to 54.7%, validating that our P-FAB reinforcement learning stage effectively refines the model’s ability to ground reasoning in precise temporal boundaries.

Superior Reasoning in General Video Understanding. The advantages of our structured-fact-guided thinking extend to broader semantic tasks. On ETBench, Factum-4B leads in six 6/86/8 sub-tasks, notably achieving 66.1% in TVG task and 26.8% in TEM task. Remarkably, our 4B model even surpasses the proprietary GPT-4o in these specific temporal-heavy tasks, proving that a structured thinking process not only improves temporal abilities, but also improves general understanding performances. This trend continues across comprehensive benchmarks: Factum-4B achieves 64.7% on VideoMME and a state-of-the-art 73.6% on NExT-GQA, representing a 7% absolute gain over the Thinking base model. On MLVU, our model maintains top-tier performance in Temporal Reasoning and Ego-centric understanding, effectively bridging the gap between open-source specialized models and multi-billion parameter proprietary systems. These combined results underscore that by enabling causal reasoning, Factum-4B achieves a more robust and granular understanding of complex video dynamics.

##### Discussion and Weaknesses.

A consistent trend across benchmarks is that unstructured thinking does not enable better video understanding ability: Qwen3-VL-4B-Thinking consistently underperforms Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct on nearly all benchmarks, aligning with our motivation that verbose reasoning can introduce evidence drift and weaken temporal alignment. In contrast, our framework explicitly organizes important clues into a compact format, leading to stronger performance and improved precision under stricter temporal thresholds. However, our current training data is still limited, there’s a need to scale up the dataset to enable the model to achieve strong performance across all video understanding tasks in the future.

##  5 Conclusion

In this paper, we present Factum-4B, a Video-LLM that bridges the cognitive gap between human video understanding and current models by introducing Structured Event Facts, serving as an explicit constraint that guides the model towards interpretable causal video reasoning. We develop a four-stage training pipeline that progressively equips the model with facts sketching and causal reasoning towards real-world task. To handle multi-objectives trade-offs under constrained length budgets during RL, we introduce Pareto-Frontier guided Advantage Balancing (P-FAB) algorithm. Empirically, Factum-4B yields more stable and reliable reasoning, delivering stronger performance on challenging video understanding tasks that demand temporal localization and causal inference. We hope this work provides a promising direction for scalable, evidence-grounded video reasoning systems.

##  Appendix A Detailed Information of Evaluation Benchmarks

To comprehensively evaluate the capabilities of our model, particularly in temporal reasoning and open-ended generation, we utilize a diverse set of benchmarks categorized into two primary domains: Video Temporal Grounding and General Video Understanding.

###  A.1 Video Temporal Grounding Benchmarks

These benchmarks evaluate the model’s ability to precisely localize specific moments within untrimmed videos based on textual queries.

  * •

Charades-TimeLens (Zhang et al., 2025a): A high-quality dataset re-annotated based on the Charades-STA dataset, where 66.3% of low-quality annotations were rewritten. The final dataset contains 1,313 videos and 3,363 annotations, with an average video duration of 29.3 seconds. This benchmark focuses on daily indoor activities (e.g., cooking, reading) and is characterized by dense actions and complex temporal overlaps. Unlike the noisy annotations in the original dataset, Charades-TimeLens provides rigorously verified timestamps to evaluate fine-grained temporal localization. We report Recall@1 at IoU thresholds of 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7.

  * •

ActivityNet-TimeLens (Zhang et al., 2025a): A re-annotated version of the ActivityNet-Captions (Krishna et al., 2017) dataset introduced in the TimeLens benchmark, where 69.7% of low-quality annotations were rewritten. The final dataset contains 1455 videos and 4500 annoations, with and average video duration of 134.9s. It addresses the issue of loose boundaries in the original annotations by providing stricter and more precise start/end times for temporal queries. Evaluating on this split offers a more rigorous assessment of a model’s boundary alignment capabilities.

  * •

ActivityNet-Captions (Krishna et al., 2017): A large-scale Video Temporal Grounding benchmark containing 4885 open-domain YouTube videos for evaluation, with 17031 annotations and an average video duration of 118s. It covers a wide range of complex human activities. The dataset is widely used for dense video captioning and temporal grounding. We evaluate performance using Recall@1 at IoU thresholds of 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7.




###  A.2 General Video Understanding Benchmarks

These benchmarks assess the model’s holistic understanding, including temporal logic, causal reasoning, and ego-centric perception.

  * •

VideoMME (Fu et al., 2024): A comprehensive Multi-modal LLM Evaluation benchmark designed for long-form video understanding. It covers diverse domains including movies, sports, and documentaries, with video lengths ranging from minutes to hours. It assesses the model’s ability to process long-context information without subtitles.

  * •

MLVU (Zhou et al., 2025): A holistic benchmark specifically tailored for long videos. It includes diverse tasks such as Topic Reasoning, Anomaly Recognition, Egocentric Video Understanding QA, etc. In our experiments, we specifically focus on the Temporal Reasoning (TR) and Egocentric Video Understanding (Ego) sub-tasks to evaluate the model’s sequence understanding and first-person perspective reasoning.

  * •

ETBench (Liu et al., 2024b): A fine-grained benchmark designed to evaluate time-sensitive video understanding. We test the model on 8 different tasks: Referred Action Recognition (RAR), Event-Caption Alignment (ECA), Refferred Video Question-Answering (RVQ), Temporal Video Grounding (TVG), Episodic Memory (EPM), Temporal Action Localization (TAL), Temporal Event Matching (TEM), and Grounded Video Question-Answering (GVQ). This benchmark is crucial for verifying whether the model’s thinking process translates to accurate event-level deductions.

  * •

NExT-GQA (Xiao et al., 2024): An extension of the NExT-QA dataset that introduces grounded video question answering. It focuses on causal (Why) and temporal (How) questions involving rich object interactions. Unlike standard QA, NExT-GQA requires the model to not only answer the question but also ground the answer in specific video segments, making it an ideal testbed for our reasoning-based approach.

###  B.4 Data Curation Prompts

This section describes the prompts used in our data curation pipeline. We employ three specialized prompts for different stages: Facts Generation, Quality Judge, and Thinking Generation. The complete prompts are illustrated in Figure 9, Figure 10, and Figure 11, respectively.

##### Facts Generation Prompt.

The Facts Generation prompt (Figure 9) instructs the model to analyze video content and produce structured summaries for pre-defined time segments. Given a list of timestamps as input, the model generates a theme summary and event factuals for each segment. The prompt enforces a strict schema with six required components per segment: [person], [human_action], [scene], [object], [camera], and [casual_event_caption]. A critical dependency rule ensures logical consistency: if no person is present in the scene, the human action field must be marked as “None”. The prompt also specifies that timestamps must be preserved in their original seconds format without conversion to MM:SS, and all output must be enclosed within <facts></facts> tags.

##### Quality Judge Prompt.

The Quality Judge prompt (Figure 10) defines a strict evaluation protocol for validating generated facts. It takes two inputs: the target timestamps that must be present and the model-generated structured text. The evaluation criteria are organized into four categories: (1) Format Adherence ensures proper tag structure, required headers, and all six mandatory fields in every line; (2) Timestamp Alignment verifies one-to-one correspondence between output lines and input segments, rejecting any missing or hallucinated timestamps; (3) Logical Consistency enforces the person-action dependency rule, ensuring that “None” person entries always correspond to “None” actions; and (4) Content Quality assesses completeness, fluency of captions, and specificity of descriptions. The judge outputs a structured JSON containing a numeric score (0-10), a regeneration flag, a list of critical errors, and qualitative feedback.

##### Thinking Generation Prompt.

The Thinking Generation prompt (Figure 11) guides the model to produce evidence-based reasoning chains for video question answering. Given a user question and extracted factuals, the model must derive answers through a two-stage structured reasoning process. In Stage 1: Global Search & Localization, the model scans factual descriptions for keywords related to the question, identifies candidate time segments based solely on textual evidence, and explicitly states which factual IDs are selected as candidates. In Stage 2: Causal Verification, the model validates candidates using antecedent-action-consequence logic: [Antecedent] establishes the pre-condition contrast, [Verifying Key Visual Actions] provides textual proof of the event, and [Consequence] confirms the post-condition result. The output follows a <thinking>...</thinking><answer>...</answer> format, ensuring that reasoning is grounded in explicit factual evidence rather than implicit assumptions.

##  Appendix C Instruction Tuning Data Format

To effectively train the model to reason over structured evidence, we design a progressive prompt engineering strategy that guides the generation of training data across three distinct stages. This multi-stage approach ensures the model first learns to extract high-density visual facts, then adapts to a strict structural format, and finally integrates these skills into a coherent reasoning chain.

In the initial stage, the objective is to align the model’s visual perception with our structured event schema, as displayed in Figure 12. We explicitly instruct the model to localize activity events and generate a detailed report containing a global theme and detailed event factuals. We enforce a rigid output format for each temporal segment, requiring specific fields such as time, person, human action, scene, object, camera, and a casual event caption. This design forces the model to decompose complex video dynamics into discrete, verifiable atomic elements rather than generating generic captions, effectively transforming raw video data into the structured representation used throughout the pipeline.

Before tackling complex reasoning tasks, the model must internalize the specific XML-like tag structure required for our framework. We therefore introduce a format warm-up stage, as shown in Figure 13. The system prompt defines the interaction protocol: the assistant must first output the factual block, followed by a thinking block, and conclude with the answering block. Since the task here is simply to reproduce video facts, the thinking block is populated with a trivial placeholder. This step ensures the model adheres to the syntactic structure without the cognitive load of complex problem-solving.

The final stage focuses on generating high-quality chain-of-thought data, as depicted in Figure 14. We combine the structured facts from the first stage with challenging QA pairs. The prompt requires the model to first recall the structured factual context. Crucially, the subsequent thinking block is no longer trivial; it contains a detailed derivation process where the model performs global search, causal verification, and alignment based on the provided facts. This stage produces the gold-standard thinking data used to warm-start the policy model, ensuring that the reasoning process is explicitly grounded in the extracted evidence.

##  Appendix D More details of Reinforcement Learning

###  D.1 Detailed Rewards Design

To guide the model toward generating structured, causally grounded, and concise reasoning chains, we design a composite reward function consisting of three distinct components: Format Reward, Linear IoU Reward, Multi-choice Accuracy Reward, and Length Reward.

##### Causal Thinking Format Reward (rfmtr_{\text{fmt}}).

To enforce the structured reasoning schema proposed in our method, we design a rule-based format reward. This reward evaluates whether the model strictly follows the <factual>, <thinking>, and <answering> XML structure and whether the <thinking> block explicitly traverses the required causal reasoning steps.

Let 𝒦\mathcal{K} be the set of mandatory causal keywords defined as:

| 𝒦={\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=\\{ | “Global Search”, “Causal Verification”, “Final Alignment”, |  | (6)  
---|---|---|---|---  
|  | “Antecedent”, “Visual Verification”, “Consequence”}\displaystyle\textit{``Antecedent'', ``Visual Verification'', ``Consequence''}\\} |   
  
Let 𝒫basic\mathcal{P}_{\text{basic}} denote the satisfaction of the basic regex pattern, 𝒯valid\mathcal{T}_{\text{valid}} denote the constraint that all XML tags appear exactly once, and 𝒞think\mathcal{C}_{\text{think}} represent the content extracted from the <thinking> block. The format reward rfmtr_{\text{fmt}} is strictly defined as:

| rfmt={1.0if ​𝒫basic∧𝒯valid∧(𝒦⊆𝒞think)0.5if ​𝒫basic∧[¬𝒯valid∨(𝒦⊈𝒞think)]0.0otherwise (pattern mismatch)r_{\text{fmt}}=\begin{cases}1.0&\text{if }\mathcal{P}_{\text{basic}}\land\mathcal{T}_{\text{valid}}\land(\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\text{think}})\\\ 0.5&\text{if }\mathcal{P}_{\text{basic}}\land[\neg\mathcal{T}_{\text{valid}}\lor(\mathcal{K}\not\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\text{think}})]\\\ 0.0&\text{otherwise (pattern mismatch)}\end{cases} |  | (7)  
---|---|---|---  
  
This reward scheme assigns a baseline score of 0.5 for adhering to the basic format structure, grants a full score of 1.0 only when the reasoning chain is structurally complete and causally rigorous, and assigns 0.0 for format violations.

##### Task-Specific Performance Rewards (rtaskr_{\text{task}}).

Depending on the downstream task type, we apply different performance metrics as rewards:

Linear IoU Reward): For temporal grounding tasks, we compute the Temporal Intersection over Union (TIoU) between the predicted time segments Sp​r​e​dS_{pred} and ground truth segments Sg​tS_{gt}. To handle ambiguity where a single continuous prediction covers multiple disjoint ground truth segments, we implement a hybrid scoring mechanism. Specifically, if |Sp​r​e​d|=1|S_{pred}|=1 and |Sg​t|>1|S_{gt}|>1, the reward is defined as max⁡(Coverage Ratio,Span IoU)\max(\text{Coverage Ratio},\text{Span IoU}), ensuring the model is rewarded for correctly identifying the overall event span. Otherwise, we compute the average IoU across all segment pairs.

Multi-choice Accuracy Reward: For QA tasks, we strictly parse the predicted option (A/B/C/D) from the <answering> block. The reward is a binary indicator function: racc=𝕀​(Pred=GT)r_{\text{acc}}=\mathbb{I}(\text{Pred}=\text{GT}).

##### Length Reward (rlenr_{\text{len}}).

To prevent the “verbosity tax” often associated with Chain-of-Thought reasoning, we introduce a soft length constraint. Unlike hard truncation, we employ a piecewise linear decay function to gently discourage excessive token generation beyond a target budget.

Let LL be the generated response length, Lm​a​xL_{max} be the hard maximum length, and Lb​u​f​f​e​rL_{buffer} be a soft buffer zone. The target length is defined as Lt​a​r​g​e​t=Lm​a​x−Lb​u​f​f​e​rL_{target}=L_{max}-L_{buffer}. The efficiency reward is calculated as:

| rlen​(L)={1.0if ​L≤Lt​a​r​g​e​t1.0−L−Lt​a​r​g​e​tLb​u​f​f​e​rif ​Lt​a​r​g​e​t<L≤Lm​a​x0.0if ​L>Lm​a​xr_{\text{len}}(L)=\begin{cases}1.0&\text{if }L\leq L_{target}\\\ 1.0-\frac{L-L_{target}}{L_{buffer}}&\text{if }L_{target}<L\leq L_{max}\\\ 0.0&\text{if }L>L_{max}\end{cases} |  | (8)

This design provides a full reward for concise answers within the target budget and applies a linear penalty only when the model enters the buffer zone, preventing the optimization instability caused by abrupt reward cliffs.

###  D.2 Frank-Wolfe Algorithm for Solving the Minimum-Norm Problem

To solve the minimum-norm optimization problem in Eq. (3.3), we employ the Frank-Wolfe algorithm, also known as the conditional gradient method (Jaggi, 2013), which is particularly well-suited for constrained convex optimization over polytopes such as the probability simplex.

##### Problem Formulation.

Recall that our objective is to find the optimal weight vector 𝜶∗\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*} that minimizes the squared norm of the aggregated direction in the standardized space:

| 𝜶∗=arg⁡min𝜶∈ΔM⁡f​(𝜶),wheref​(𝜶)=‖𝐃^q​𝜶‖2.\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta_{M}}f(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\quad\text{where}\quad f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\left\|\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\|^{2}. |  | (9)  
---|---|---|---  
  
This is a convex quadratic program over the simplex ΔM={𝜶∈ℝM∣∑m=1Mαm=1,αm≥0}\Delta_{M}=\\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\mid\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}=1,\alpha_{m}\geq 0\\}.

##### Algorithm Description.

Starting from a uniform initialization 𝜶(0)=1M​𝟏M\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{1}_{M}, each iteration tt proceeds as follows:

Step 1: Gradient Computation. We compute the gradient of the objective at the current iterate:

| ∇f​(𝜶(t))=2​𝐃^q⊤​(𝐃^q​𝜶(t)).\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)})=2\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}^{\top}(\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)}). |  | (10)  
---|---|---|---  
  
Step 2: Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO). We solve a linear minimization problem over the simplex:

| 𝐬(t)=arg⁡min𝐬∈ΔM⁡⟨𝐬,∇f​(𝜶(t))⟩.\mathbf{s}^{(t)}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{s}\in\Delta_{M}}\langle\mathbf{s},\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)})\rangle. |  | (11)  
---|---|---|---  
  
Since ΔM\Delta_{M} is a simplex, the solution is simply a one-hot vector placing all mass on the coordinate with the smallest gradient component:

| 𝐬(t)=𝐞k∗,wherek∗=argminm[∇f(𝜶(t))]m.\mathbf{s}^{(t)}=\mathbf{e}_{k^{*}},\quad\text{where}\quad k^{*}=\arg\min_{m}[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)})]_{m}. |  | (12)  
---|---|---|---  
  
Step 3: Exact Line Search. We compute the update direction 𝐝(t)=𝐬(t)−𝜶(t)\mathbf{d}^{(t)}=\mathbf{s}^{(t)}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)} and determine the optimal step size via exact line search. For the quadratic objective, this admits a closed-form solution:

| γ(t)=max⁡(0,min⁡(1,−⟨𝐃^q​𝜶(t),𝐃^q​𝐝(t)⟩‖𝐃^q​𝐝(t)‖2)).\gamma^{(t)}=\max\left(0,\min\left(1,\frac{-\langle\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)},\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}\mathbf{d}^{(t)}\rangle}{\|\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}\mathbf{d}^{(t)}\|^{2}}\right)\right). |  | (13)  
---|---|---|---  
  
If ‖𝐃^q​𝐝(t)‖2<ϵ\|\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}\mathbf{d}^{(t)}\|^{2}<\epsilon (we use ϵ=10−12\epsilon=10^{-12}), we terminate early to avoid numerical instability.

Step 4: Weight Update. We update the weights via:

| 𝜶(t+1)=𝜶(t)+γ(t)​𝐝(t).\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t+1)}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)}+\gamma^{(t)}\mathbf{d}^{(t)}. |  | (14)  
---|---|---|---  
  
##### Termination Criteria.

The algorithm terminates when the change in weights falls below a tolerance threshold ‖𝜶(t+1)−𝜶(t)‖<τ\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t+1)}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)}\|<\tau (we use τ=10−6\tau=10^{-6}), or when the maximum number of iterations is reached (we use Tmax=50T_{\max}=50).

##### Convergence Guarantees.

The exact line search ensures monotonic decrease of the objective value at every iteration, guaranteeing convergence to the global optimum of this convex problem. In the context of multi-objective optimization, the solution corresponds to a Pareto-stationary point.
